Best Practices and Techniques

It is not simply handing off tasks to reduce workload; it is a deliberate way to develop people, build capability within the team, and ensure work is delivered at the right level. When done properly, it strengthens both performance and accountability. When done poorly, it creates confusion, rework, and frustration. The starting point is clarity. Delegation must come with a clear outcome, not just an instruction.

Instead of telling someone what to do step by step, a strong manager explains what success looks like, the expected standard, and any constraints such as deadlines or dependencies. This gives the individual direction while still allowing them ownership over how the work is completed. Without this clarity, people either guess or constantly seek reassurance, which defeats the purpose of delegating. Trust is central to delegation. A manager must be willing to let go of control over how something is done, while still remaining accountable for the result.

This is often where delegation breaks down. If a manager continues to interfere or redo the work, it signals a lack of trust and discourages initiative. At the same time, delegation is not abandonment. The manager should remain available for guidance and check in at appropriate points, especially for more complex tasks. Matching the task to the individual is equally important. Delegation should consider skill level, experience, and development needs. Routine tasks can be assigned to build confidence and consistency, while more challenging work can stretch individuals and support growth.

The goal is to gradually increase capability, not to overload or set someone up to fail. Understanding each team member’s strengths and gaps allows delegation to be both effective and developmental. Communication during delegation needs to be two-way. After assigning the task, the manager should confirm understanding. Asking the individual to explain their approach or next steps ensures alignment and highlights any gaps early. This avoids situations where work is completed incorrectly due to misunderstanding, which often leads to frustration on both sides.

Accountability must remain clear. Delegating a task does not remove the manager’s responsibility; it shifts the execution to someone else while the manager retains ownership of the outcome. This means setting clear checkpoints, agreeing on updates, and ensuring progress is visible. If something starts to go off track, it can be corrected early rather than at the end. Feedback completes the delegation cycle. Once the task is finished, it is important to review what went well and what could be improved. This reinforces learning and builds confidence. Positive outcomes should be recognised, and any issues should be addressed constructively.

Without feedback, delegation becomes transactional rather than developmental. Common mistakes in delegation include giving unclear instructions, delegating only low-value tasks, or taking back control too quickly when things are not perfect. Another common issue is uneven delegation, where some team members are overloaded while others are underutilised. This creates imbalance and limits overall team effectiveness. Done right, delegation creates a more capable and engaged team. It allows the manager to focus on higher-level responsibilities while building strength within the team. Over time, it reduces dependency on the manager and creates an environment where individuals take ownership of their work and contribute more confidently to the overall success of the team.

The best executive is the one who has sense enough to pick good people to do what he wants done.

— Theodore Roosevelt
